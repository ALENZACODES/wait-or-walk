## Overview
**Subway stopped? Paste the TTC alert to see the chance of a long delay, timetabled buses nearby, and whether to wait.** Wait or Walk answers in about 30 seconds: alert, chance, Plan B, verdict. Paste the TTC alert (or tap one from the copied TTC feed) and it gives the estimated chance that comparable recorded delays lasted 5, 10 or 20 minutes or more, the reasons behind it, and scheduled buses, streetcars and LRT within 300 metres that head where you are going, ordered by the arrival the timetable allows, counting only scheduled departures you may be able to catch (timetable estimates, not real time), and says whether waiting or switching looks better by the timetable, with the break even margin. No rider testing has been done yet.

## Problem and solution
TTC alerts say what happened, not how long it will take. Riders guess, and guessing wrong costs real time: people wait twenty minutes for a fire investigation to clear, or leave a platform for a passenger alarm that would have cleared in three. City of Toronto Open Data publishes a TTC subway delay log starting January 1, 2025, with each incident's cause, station and minutes of delay; the copy used here has 47,915 records up to September 30, 2026. Wait or Walk turns those records into a risk estimate you can use on the platform, for daily commuters, students, shift workers and visitors who do not know the surface routes.

## How it works
- **Reading the alert:** you paste the whole alert (there is a paste button and a link to TTC service alerts), or tap one from a copy of the TTC's open GTFS Realtime subway alerts (City of Toronto Open Data, Open Government Licence Toronto). The page cannot read that feed itself (it sends no cross origin header), so a scheduled GitHub Action (`.github/workflows/alerts.yml`, `scripts/fetch_alerts.py`) copies it into `data/live_alerts.json` every 30 minutes and commits only when the alerts changed; the page shows when the copy was made and paste always works. The job also logs when each alert was first and last seen (`data/alert_history.json`), shown as an "alerts seen this month" strip: a record of what the TTC posted, not a check of the forecasts. The page asks GitHub's public API when the job last ran; if the copy was last checked more than 90 minutes ago it marks the alerts as possibly out of date, in red, with a link to TTC service alerts. The job's commits are automated: they are titled "Copy TTC subway alerts", are made under my name by the workflow, and change only the alerts data files (`data/live_alerts.json`, `data/alert_history.json` and `data/access_alerts.json`) and the alert time forecast log (`data/alert_forecasts.jsonl`). The job runs on a 30 minute schedule and can also be started by hand. Scheduled runs: GitHub has started the job on its own once so far, at 6:53 p.m. Toronto time on November 15, 2026 (run 43777263323, which committed a new copy); the three earlier runs were started by hand. Over the next few hours no other scheduled run fired, although the schedule asks for one every 30 minutes. The cron line is valid, the workflow is active and on the default branch, and the one scheduled run shows the trigger works; GitHub documents that scheduled runs can be delayed or dropped when its runners are busy, and a newly added or edited schedule can take a while to settle. The page does not depend on it: it reads when the job last ran and marks the copy as possibly out of date after 90 minutes, and paste always works. Each run also saves the forecast for every new alert at that time (`data/alert_forecasts.jsonl`, see Honest results). Alerts that read as planned work or a slow zone get no forecast at all: "This is planned service work. The incident delay model does not apply." It is read by hand written rules, not a trained model. Regular expressions find line names ("Line 2", "Bloor-Danforth"), keyword patterns find 14 causes in a fixed order with the most specific first ("person at track level" before "track"), and a search finds the 71 station names plus a few older names, such as Dundas for TMU. Values read are tagged "from alert, please check".
- **No number until it is clear:** two stations named, a two line station without a line, or a station that is not on the chosen line (Union on Line 2) all give no number until you choose. Clearing the alert clears what it filled in. Automated tests cover each case.
- **Three calibrated models:** logistic regressions for 5+, 10+ and 20+ minutes on cause (the TTC's 124 delay codes grouped into the 15 causes alerts use), station, line, time of day and weekend, trained in Python with scikit-learn and run in the browser, so nothing you type is sent anywhere. The three are kept in order; before that step they crossed for under 1% of test delays. A parity test checks the browser against Python on 30 inputs for all three.
- **Drift:** long delays became rarer in 2026 (about 19% of delays in early 2025, about 12% by summer 2026). Newer delays count more (180 day half life), and each output is recalibrated with Platt scaling on the latest 91 days; the base model never sees those days, and that exact pair ships with no refit. The call card shows the forecast before and after.
- **Already underway.** Three buttons on the card: "Just started", "Delay already reached 5 min" and "Delay already reached 10 min", with the note "The model is calibrated on recorded delay length, not time since the alert." They condition the same calibrated models, with no refit: P(10+ | 5+) = P(10+) / P(5+) and P(20+ | 10+) = P(20+) / P(10+), which stay between 0 and 1 because the bands are kept in order (zero bands give no number rather than a division by zero). Expected minutes left come from the averages of the ranges still possible, minus the minutes gone. The log gives total minutes, not alert times, so the buttons say what they mean: "the recorded delay already reached 5 minutes". Checked on the same test periods (`scripts/underway_checks.py`): among 3,573 test delays that reached 5 minutes, the 10+ chance averaged 34% against 31% actual (AUC 0.689, 0.671 to 0.707, against 0.678 for the cause alone; calibration error 3.2 points); among 1,092 that reached 10, the 20+ chance averaged 28% against 28% actual (28.5 against 28.3) (AUC 0.683, 0.644 to 0.720). Expected minutes left against logged: 6.9 against 6.6 from the start, 5.4 against 5.0 from 5 minutes, 9.1 against 8.9 from 10 minutes.
- **Wait or switch:** the card turns the three bands into an expected logged delay and compares it with the best scheduled switch to your destination (its arrival minus an on time subway arrival). It suggests switching only when switching still wins by at least 5 minutes, so that it still wins with the bus 5 minutes late (the late bus check, tested on earlier and later data; see Honest results). When switching wins by less, the card says "Narrow margin" and says wait; there is no "too close to call" answer. A break even line says by how many minutes switching wins or loses, and the timetable risk lines show the same trip with the bus on time, 5 minutes late, and missed.
- **Plan B, scheduled alternatives:** from the TTC schedule (files dated Oct 20, 2026, covering Nov 1 to Dec 21, 2026, the newest published on November 15), every regular stop pattern of every bus, streetcar and LRT route with the subway stations it passes in order. Only scheduled departures you may be able to catch are shown (timetable estimates, not real time): leaving at or after now plus the walk (80 metres a minute in a straight line) plus 1 minute to board. Entries are ordered by that departure plus the scheduled ride, plus the scheduled subway time onward where you change, never claimed as the quickest way. Times refresh every minute and follow the service date past midnight; outside the schedule's dates the page says "Schedule data ends on Dec 21, 2026" and shows no times. A warning above the list says a scheduled alternative might not be running now. Five journeys were checked by hand against the raw schedule files. Where the TTC's older NextBus feed (on UmoIQ, open to browsers with no key) has a live prediction for a Plan B stop, route and direction, the page shows it under the scheduled times, marked live, with the time it was checked; where it has none, or cannot be read, the page says the times are timetable estimates. The City lists that feed as decommissioned, so it may stop; the newer TTC GTFS Realtime feed cannot be read from a browser. Not shown on the simulated examples. The verdict still uses the timetable.
- **Plan B extras, behind a fold.** An elevator or escalator outage at the delay station or your destination shows as a warning under Plan B, from the TTC's accessibility alerts feed, copied by the same job (Open Government Licence Toronto); the page does not invent step free routes. Inside "More: route delay history and Bike Share": how often each Plan B route logged a delay of 10 minutes or more (TTC bus and streetcar delay logs, Jan to Sep 2026, per scheduled trip), shown only because a check passed first: each route's 2025 rate ranked the routes' 2026 rates well (rank correlation 0.87 over 200 routes with at least 200 trips a week) and was a closer guess than the network average (average error 0.08 against 0.17 per 100 trips). These count reported incidents, not on time performance, and do not change the estimate. Then Bike Share Toronto stations within 400 metres of the delay station and the destination, with live bikes and open docks read in your browser from the Bike Share Toronto GBFS feed (no ride time is estimated; snow, luggage or a stroller can make cycling the wrong choice). The weather panel of the last version was cut: it was not part of the forecast and had not been tested as a predictor.

## Honest results
**In brief:**
1. **Ranking:** better than the cause alone (AUC 0.751 against 0.727) and level with the first model (0.751 against 0.753, not distinguishable).
2. **Calibration:** expected calibration error 1.8 points pooled, against 2.4 for the first model (distinguishable). Calibration in the large (average forecast against actual share of 10+ delays) by period: 18% against 18%, 17% against 14% and 14% against 12%, so forecasts ran high in 2026 as long delays kept getting rarer. A fresher recalibration, fitted on earlier data and checked on later, did not pass and is not shipped.
3. **Decisions:** the card switches only when switching still wins with the bus 5 minutes late. In simulation, as scheduled, that saves 0.06 minutes per delay over all delays (0.04 to 0.08) and about 1 minute per delay of 20 minutes or more (0.95, 0.54 to 1.43). The timetable only rule it replaces saved more when buses ran on time (0.16, and 3.08 on 20+ minute delays) but lost time when they did not; averaged over eight stress scenarios the late bus check is 0.63 minutes per delay better (0.55 to 0.70), and the earlier windows agree. A missed bus still erases the gain. Exploratory.
4. **Reading alerts:** on 102 real TTC alerts with hand set labels, the first version named the right station in 87% and the right cause in 92%. Fixes found on the 51 older alerts lifted the 51 newer ones to 98% for both. After three more fixes all 102 are right, on the alerts used to fix it.
5. **Live times:** where the TTC's older NextBus feed has a live prediction for a Plan B stop, it is shown beside the timetable; everything else is a timetable estimate, and the verdict uses the timetable. No rider testing has been done; a five question test script is ready in docs/rider-test.md.

**Drift in the expected delay.** The averages in each range come from the base training data, and long delays became rarer in 2026. They barely moved: for 20+ minutes, 33.9 minutes in the base data against 32.0, 35.2 and 34.0 in the windows just before each period. Using those recent averages instead (with the margin picked again on the same earlier window) changed the simulated minutes by 0.01 per delay, not distinguishable, so the shipped averages are kept. The expected delay does run high in the later periods (6.4 against 5.95 logged minutes on average in Jul to Sep, 7.1 against 6.4 in Apr to Jun) because the forecasts themselves run high, not because of the averages.

**Forecast log, a plan.** `docs/forecast-log.md` gets one dated line each time the City publishes newer delays: the frozen shipped model scored, never refit, on delays after September 30, 2026, against both baselines (`scripts/forecast_log.py`). Today it has one line: no new delays yet, so no score.

**Alert time forecast log.** Each run of the alerts job now also saves the forecast for every new subway alert as the page would have shown it then (time of day, the alert as read, the 5+, 10+ and 20+ chances, the model it came from), one line per alert in `data/alert_forecasts.jsonl`, never edited (`scripts/log_forecasts.mjs`). Lines logged more than 40 minutes after the alert was first seen are marked backfilled; the first two are. Once the City publishes delays for those dates they can be matched and scored. It has not been scored, so it makes no accuracy claim.

**Why forecasts run high, and a fix that did not pass.** Two measures, named: calibration in the large is the average forecast against the actual share; calibration error is the expected calibration error, the gap in seven forecast bands weighted by delays. The pooled 1.8 points hides the direction: by period the 10+ forecasts averaged 18% against 18% actual (Jan to Mar), 17% against 14% (Apr to Jun) and 14% against 12% (Jul to Sep); in the 5 minute underway check, 34% against 31%. The share of 10+ delays kept falling through 2026 (19% in January, 15% in April, 11% in July), and the recalibration averages the 91 days before each period, so it lags a falling trend. Tested (`scripts/calibration_check.py`): shift each period's forecasts so they match the actual share in only the last 30, 46 or 61 days of its earlier window, then score on the later period. Rule: ship only if the 10+ gap shrinks in all three periods and pooled log loss is no worse for any band; this rule was written after a first look at the numbers. None passed. Each window helps Apr to Jun (17.3% down to 15.1% to 16.6%, against 13.7% actual) but pushes Jan to Mar off (18.0% to 17.0% to 19.1%, against 18.0%) and leaves Jul to Sep about where it was (13.4% to 13.7%, against 12.0%). So the forecasts ship as they are, with this note.

**Reading the alert, tested.** 102 real TTC alerts posted from August to November 2026, each labelled by hand from the text alone (`data/alerts_labelled.json`, scored by `scripts/parser_eval.mjs`, which runs in CI): the first version named the right station in 87% (13 wrong) and the right cause in 92% (99% of the causes it named were right), and caught 6 of 7 planned work alerts. Fixes found on the 51 older alerts (a station name inside a longer one, such as St Clair in St Clair West; "Vaughan"; "operational problem", "communication issue" and "TPS investigation") lifted the 51 newer ones from 82% to 98% for stations and from 91% to 98% for causes. Three more fixes for the errors left in the newer half make all 102 right, but that score is on the alerts used to fix it, so it is not an independent test. Station counts as right when the one station named is read, or, for an alert naming two or more, when the choice lists exactly those stations.

## Responsible use
No accounts and no tracking; the last destination you chose is kept only in your own browser, and "Copy a link to this result" puts the alert in the link itself, not on any server. Not affiliated with or endorsed by the Toronto Transit Commission. Always follow TTC staff and announcements; it is not for decisions about leaving a train or evacuating. The forecast is labelled as an estimate, rare causes and stations are flagged, every value read from an alert can be checked and changed, and Plan B is labelled as scheduled alternatives that might not be running. The alert is pasted, or taken from a copy of the TTC feed made every 30 minutes, which can be late, and the page marks it as possibly out of date after 90 minutes; planned work and slow zones get no forecast. The "Copy TTC subway alerts" commits in the repo are automated and change only the alerts data files and the alert time forecast log. No manual screen reader test has been done; the accessibility checks are automated. Blue Night routes are listed only overnight. No rider testing has been done; a five question test script is ready in docs/rider-test.md.

## Challenges I ran into
The drift was the big one: a model that looked fine on 2025 data was wrong on 2026, and my first fix used a calibration process that did not match the shipped model, which I caught and redid. Matching station names was the other: the delay log writes "BLOOR STATION", "YONGE BD STATION" and old names like "DUNDAS", while the schedule uses "Bloor Station", "Yonge Station" and "TMU Station". And the simulation first let crosstown buses "beat" the subway by jumping across the U of Line 1; limiting it to stations a few stops along fixed that.

## What I learned
Calibration matters as much as ranking for a tool people act on, it has to be validated the same way it is shipped, and intervals keep you honest about which differences are real. Here the cause alone gets most of the way, and the time saved is in the long tail. A decision rule needs the same care as the model: picking its setting on earlier data and testing it on later periods turned a rule that lost time overall into one that saves a little.
