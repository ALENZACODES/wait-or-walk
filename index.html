        3. Plan B: scheduled alternatives

        These are scheduled alternatives, never a promise of the quickest way. Routes, directions and stops come from the TTC schedule (files dated Oct 20, 2026, covering Nov 1 to Dec 21, 2026), using each route's regular stop patterns; a stop counts as near a station within 300 metres in a straight line, about a 4 minute walk. The schedule cannot tell you about detours, crowding or shuttle buses, so check TTC service alerts. Five journeys were checked by hand against the schedule; it has not been tested with riders.

        4. Verdict: wait or switch?

        Paste an alert to see the comparison.

        How good is it?

        Ranking: better than the cause alone (AUC 0.751 against 0.727) and level with the first model (0.751 against 0.753, not distinguishable).

        Calibration: better calibrated than the first model (1.8 against 2.4 points, distinguishable), though forecasts still run a few points high.

        Decisions: on delays of 20 minutes or more, the schedule comparison saves about 3 minutes per delay in simulation (3.08, 95% interval 2.23 to 4.04). Averaged over all delays the gain is small (0.16 minutes, 0.11 to 0.20), exploratory, and gone if buses run 5 minutes late or a departure is missed. It has not been tested with riders.

        Evidence: tables, checks and caveats

        Which data each test period's model saw

        The shipped model itself is never scored; every number on this page comes from per period models built the same way. No test period is scored with a calibration fit on that period. Each period has its own base model, trained on everything before its recalibration window, and its own Platt scaling, fit on the 91 days before the period starts. The shipped model is a separate fit (base model to Jul 1, 2026, recalibrated on Jul 2 to Sep 30, 2026) and is not used for any score on this page; the calibration chart also uses the test period forecasts. An automated test checks that every recalibration window ends before its test period starts.

        Jul to Sep 2026 is not a fully independent check. An earlier private prototype was tested on that period, and what it showed (forecasts running high) led to the age weighting and recalibration. So results from that period informed the approach. For that reason the pooled results are also shown without it, below.

        No fresh frozen test yet. On November 15, 2026 the newest TTC delay file on City of Toronto Open Data (updated October 26, 2026) still ends on September 30, 2026, the last day the shipped model saw, so there are no newer delays to score. When the City publishes newer delays, scripts/frozen_test.py scores the frozen shipped model once on them against the cause only rule and the first model. That period will be the untouched one.

        What the number means. The TTC logs each subway incident with the minutes it delayed service. In full: among comparable recorded TTC delays, the model estimates the chance the recorded delay lasts 10 minutes or more (23% in the Kipling example). This is not the chance your current alert causes that delay, or your remaining wait, which also depends on where you are and how service is restored. The chips beside it give the same for 5 and 20 minutes. With “about 5 minutes in” the headline becomes the chance of 10 minutes or more in total among comparable delays that reached 5 (the 10+ chance divided by the 5+ chance), and with “about 10 minutes in” the chance of 20 or more among those that reached 10. Records with 0 minutes of delay ( of them) are left out.

        The data. City of Toronto Open Data publishes a TTC subway delay log starting January 1, 2025. The copy used here has 47,915 records up to September 30, 2026; 16,548 of them are on Lines 1, 2 and 4 with more than 0 minutes of delay. On November 15, 2026 both this log and the TTC schedule were still the newest versions published.

        How it was made. Three logistic regressions, one each for 5+, 10+ and 20+ minutes, using only what an alert tells you: the cause, the station, the line, the time and the day. Newer delays count more (180 day half life). Each base model is trained on everything up to , then its output is recalibrated (Platt scaling) on the next three months () and shipped as is, with no refit. The three are kept in order (5+ at least the 10+ chance, 20+ at most); before that step they crossed for under 1% of test delays.

        How the settings were chosen. The 180 day half life, the 91 day recalibration window and the 15% and 35% cut offs were set in the first commit and never changed. They were not chosen blind to the test periods: an earlier private prototype was tested on July to September 2026, one of the three evaluation periods, and its forecasts ran high there, which is why age weighting and recalibration were added. No record shows other values being tried before the first commit. The table under “More checks” shows the alternatives on the same periods: a 61 day window would have scored slightly better (log loss 0.3619 against 0.3628). The shipped values were kept rather than tuned on the test periods. The 15% and 35% cut offs still set the risk labels; since version r6 the wait or switch suggestion comes from the expected cost rule described below.

        How the alert is read. You paste the whole alert (there is a paste button), or pick one from the copy of the TTC's open GTFS Realtime subway alerts that a scheduled job saves into this site every 30 minutes when they change (the TTC's feeds cannot be read from other websites directly). Alerts that read as planned work or a slow zone get no forecast. It is read by hand written rules, not a trained model: regular expressions for line names (such as “Line 2” or “Bloor-Danforth”), keyword patterns for 14 causes checked in a fixed order with the most specific first (“person at track level” before “track”), and a search for the 71 station names plus a few older names, such as Dundas for TMU. If more than one station, or a two line station without a line, could be meant, you choose.

        The baselines. Cause only rule: the recent share of long delays for the cause, recalibrated the same way. First model: the same features and regression, trained on all earlier data with equal weights and no recalibration.

        Why this model ships. Pooled over three out of time evaluation periods not used for fitting (7,455 delays), it ranks incidents as well as the first model (AUC 0.751 against 0.753, not distinguishable) with the same log loss (0.3628 against 0.3630) and a lower calibration error (1.8 against 2.4 points; 0.6 points lower, 95% interval 0.3 to 0.9). In the latest period its log loss, Brier score and calibration error are distinguishably better, because it follows the 2026 drop in long delays; in the first period (Jan to Mar 2026) it is distinguishably behind the first model on AUC, log loss and Brier score. The cause alone gets most of the way: the cause only rule reaches AUC 0.727, and station, line, time and day add 0.024 (95% interval 0.016 to 0.032).

        Without Jul to Sep 2026 (Jan to Jun only, 5,207 delays)

        On the two periods that did not inform the approach, Wait or Walk still clearly beats the cause only rule (AUC 0.742 against 0.707), but the first model is slightly ahead on AUC (0.746, by 0.004), log loss (by 0.0025) and Brier score (by 0.0008).         Intervals come from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of the delays within each period. “Not distinguishable” means the 95% interval of the difference includes zero. Calibration error is the average gap between forecast and actual across the forecast bands in the chart, weighted by the number of delays.

        Decision value at the risk band cut offs

        At 35% (high risk), Wait or Walk flags a quarter of the delays that ran 10 minutes or more, with about 5% of short delays flagged by mistake. The first model flags a few more of each (29% and 7%); the cause only rule flags fewer of each (14% and 2%). At 15% the three are close.

        The wait or switch rule, picked on earlier data

        The old rule said to check another route whenever the 10+ chance was 35% or more. Two rules were tried in its place, each with its setting picked on earlier data only (each test period's own recalibration window, which ends before the period) and then tested on that period, in the station simulation below. 20+ chance rule: switch when the 20+ minute chance reaches a threshold. Expected cost rule: switch when the expected logged delay (the three bands times the average logged minutes in each range, capped at 60) minus the station's scheduled switch cost reaches a margin. The expected cost rule was clearly better than the old rule and than always waiting, so it ships; the 20+ chance rule was not, so it does not. Average minutes lost per delay:

        Exploratory, not confirmed. The expected cost rule was chosen over the 20+ chance rule after both were compared on these same evaluation periods, so its advantage is exploratory: the intervals above do not account for that choice, and it has not been confirmed on an untouched period (none exists yet; see the forecast log). Within the rule, the margin picked on each earlier window varied: 0.5 minutes below, 1 minute above and 0.5 minutes above; the shipped margin is 0.

        Stress test: when the switch costs more than the schedule says

        Read honestly: across all delays the small gain does not survive late buses, a missed departure or longer boarding; slower walking leaves it level. On delays of 20 minutes or more it still saves time with late buses, slower walking and boarding buffers, but not with a missed departure or everything at once. That is why the card says to switch only if you can catch that bus.

        A rule picked for late buses: tested, not shipped

        Could a bigger margin protect riders when the bus runs late? For each test period the margin was picked on that period's earlier window as the one with the fewest minutes if every bus ran 5 minutes late, then the full stress test was run with it. The rule had to be at least as good as the shipped one as scheduled and lose less under slippage. Margins picked: 3.5, 12.5 and 4 minutes.

        Already underway: are the conditional chances sound?

        Drift in the expected delay. The averages in each range come from the base training data, and long delays became rarer in 2026. They barely moved: for 20+ minutes, 33.9 minutes in the base data against 32.0, 35.2 and 34.0 in the windows just before each period. Using those recent averages instead (with the margin picked again on the same earlier window) changed the simulated minutes by 0.01 per delay, not distinguishable, so the shipped averages are kept. The expected delay does run high in the later periods (6.4 against 5.95 logged minutes on average in Jul to Sep, 7.1 against 6.4 in Apr to Jun) because the forecasts themselves run high, not because of the averages.

        Read honestly: it wins by switching less on short delays (it is still 0.04 minutes behind always waiting under 10 minutes) and more on long ones. These are simulated minutes, and the intervals hold only under the simulation's assumptions: November 2026 schedule days applied to earlier delays, logged delay minutes as the cost of waiting, and alternatives that run as scheduled. On the page the switch cost uses your destination when it is on the same line; in the simulation it is the first station past the delay, averaged over both directions. That is why the Kipling example, headed to Jane 4 stops away (Islington, Royal York, Old Mill, Jane), is not limited by the simulation's cap of 3 stops: the cap only decides which station counts as past the delay in the simulation (for the 149 from Kipling, Islington, 1 stop along), while the page compares door to destination along the line. The expected delay is about logged service delays, not your own remaining wait.

        A decision simulation with schedule based switch costs, not real savings

        For each test delay and each direction along its line, switching costs the walk to the stop (80 metres a minute) plus 1 minute to board, the wait for the next scheduled departure after that, and the scheduled ride to a station past the delay (at most 3 stops along), minus the scheduled subway time between the same two stations (floored at 0).         Resampling whole days instead of single delays

        Delays on the same day can share causes such as weather, so single delay resampling may give intervals that are too narrow. As a sensitivity check, the same comparison resamples whole service days within each period:

        Chance of 5+ and 20+ minutes

        For 5+ minutes Wait or Walk ranks far better than the cause alone (AUC 0.702 against 0.643). Against the first model it is level on AUC, slightly better on Brier score (0.2192 against 0.2203) and log loss (the log loss gap holds when single delays are resampled, not when whole days are), and distinguishably better calibrated (2.4 against 4.5 points). For 20+ minutes it beats the cause alone (AUC 0.773 against 0.752) and is level with the first model on every measure. Only 4% of test delays ran 20 minutes or more, so its intervals are wide.

        Station by cause pooling: tested and rejected

        Rare combinations, such as one cause at one station, could carry their own risk. This check adds an empirical Bayes adjustment for every station and cause pair on top of the base model (observed against expected long delays, shrunk with a gamma prior whose strength is picked by marginal likelihood), then recalibrates. The marginal likelihood picks the strongest pooling tried, in effect no adjustment: the pairs add nothing the station and cause already carry. Without pooling the pairs make it worse (log loss 0.3702 against 0.3628). It is not shipped.
